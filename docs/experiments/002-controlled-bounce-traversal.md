# Experiment 002 — Controlled bounce traversal

**Status:** Implemented, awaiting a run in the Unity Editor
**Scope:** Player-controlled traversal. No enemies, combat, progression, scoring, cooldowns,
stamina, inventory, ability framework, or generalised material system.

Experiment 001 established that the two states behave differently and that a bounce can be
steered horizontally. It also produced a ceiling: two successive bounces were achievable, three
were not. This experiment asks whether that ceiling is a property of the player's control or a
property of the configuration, and then whether the mechanic can be aimed.

---

## 1. Question

Can the player intentionally use the rubber state, impact timing, momentum and horizontal
steering to reach destinations that cannot be reached by normal jumping?

## 2. Hypotheses

**H0 — The two-bounce ceiling in Experiment 001 was arithmetic, not skill.**

This has to be settled first, because every other question here is unanswerable until it is.
Experiment 001 fixed all combine modes at `Average` and gave the environment a bounciness of 0,
so the restitution actually in force was `(0.85 + 0) / 2 = 0.425`, not the 0.85 written on the
Rubber material. With `Physics.bounceThreshold` at 2 m/s, that permits **exactly two bounces**
from a 5 m drop, which is what was observed. If H0 holds, the observed difficulty was a
measurement artefact and tells us nothing about controllability.

Prediction: switching `bounceCombine` to `Maximum`, changing nothing else, takes the same drop
from 2 bounces to roughly 10.

**H1 — Horizontal momentum converts into height through angled impacts.** Restitution alone can
never gain height, because `e < 1` means every bounce returns less than it received. Climbing
therefore requires an energy source, and the cheap one is horizontal speed, which the player can
rebuild on any flat surface. An angled impact redirects it. If this works, the mechanic is not
"bounce higher" but "trade speed you have for height you want", which is a decision rather than
a stat.

**H2 — Chaining is limited by geometry, not by reflexes.** Given a surface to convert against,
a third and fourth conversion should be no harder than the first. If chaining remains hard even
with `Maximum` combine and ramps placed where the ball lands, the difficulty is real and lives
in control, not configuration.

**H3 — Switching state before impact matters, and is learnable.** Being rigid in flight and
rubber only at contact should produce a different result from being rubber throughout, because
the state at the moment of contact is what the solver reads. This is the weakest hypothesis
here and the one most likely to be wrong.

## 3. Test setup

**Scene:** `Assets/Scenes/Experiment002.unity`, generated by
`Tools > Experiment 002 > Build Traversal Chamber`. Experiment 001's scene and scripts are
untouched and remain runnable.

A three-stage vertical zigzag chamber, 38 units wide and 20 tall, all geometry in the plane
`z = 0` so the camera can view every stage side-on at once.

| Object | Position | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| `Floor` | y = 0, 38 wide | Run-up to build horizontal speed |
| `Ramp_A` | (0, 1.1), +30° | Stage 1 conversion, launches east |
| `Platform_A` | (13, 4.8), top y = 5.0 | Target A, and the run-up for stage 2 |
| `Ramp_C` | (7.5, 6.1), −30° | Stage 2 conversion, launches west |
| `Platform_B` | (−7, 9.3), top y = 9.5 | Target B, and the run-up for stage 3 |
| `Ramp_D` | (−4.5, 10.6), +30° | Stage 3 conversion, launches east |
| `Platform_C` | (10, 13.3), top y = 13.5 | Target C, the final destination |
| `Target_A/B/C` | above each platform | Invisible trigger volumes recording arrival |

**Every platform is above the reach of a normal jump.** A 6 N·s impulse on a 1 kg body gives
6 m/s, which reaches 1.83 m. The lowest target is at 5.0 m. No target can be reached by jumping,
and none can be reached without at least one ramp conversion.

**Controls:** WASD to run, Space to jump, **hold Left Shift** to be rubber, `R` to reset the
attempt, `C` to swap the bounce-combine variant.

Rubber is hold-to-activate rather than a toggle specifically so that H3 is attemptable: the
player can be rigid in flight and rubber only at the moment of contact.

## 4. Variables

**Independent:**

1. `bounceCombine` on the rubber material — `Average` (effective e = 0.425) or `Maximum`
   (effective e = 0.85). Hot-swappable with `C`. Two assets differing in exactly one field.
2. Player behaviour: approach speed, impact angle, and when the state is switched.

**Controlled:**

| Held fixed | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Bounciness | 0.85 on both rubber variants | So the variant comparison isolates combine mode alone |
| Environment material | `E2_Neutral` everywhere, bounciness 0 | Same reason as Experiment 001: unassigned colliders fall back to undocumented PhysX defaults |
| Friction combine | `Average` on every material | Only bounce combine is under test |
| Ground speed cap | 12 m/s | Fixes the energy available per conversion, so stages are comparable |
| Jump impulse | 6 N·s → 1.83 m | Defines the "unreachable by jumping" threshold every target sits above |
| Air steering force | 10 N, vs 25 N on the ground | Deliberately weak. Air control strong enough to fly would make the chamber a test of thrust rather than of bouncing |
| Mass | 1 kg | Restitution against static geometry is mass-independent |
| Collision detection | `ContinuousDynamic` | Changed from Experiment 001. Launches reach ~12 m/s = 24 cm per step against 0.4-thick platforms; `Discrete` would risk tunnelling, which would be an artefact not a result |
| `Physics.bounceThreshold` | Project default, 2 | Not overridden in this experiment |

**Explicitly not tuned:** bounciness is not raised to make the chamber easier. If a stage proves
unreachable, the platform moves. Geometry is level design; restitution is the thing under test.

## 5. Success criteria

**The mechanic proceeds to Experiment 003 if all three hold:**

1. **Reachability.** Target C is reached at least once. It requires three chained conversions,
   so reaching it is direct evidence that chaining is possible.
2. **Repeatability.** Target B is reached on a clear majority of deliberate attempts once a
   technique has been found. The exact number matters less than whether success rate *improves
   with practice* — that is the difference between skill and luck.
3. **Intent.** The player can state, before an attempt, what they are going to do, and then do
   it. A mechanic that only works by accident cannot support the "I can use it like that?"
   moment the whole direction is aimed at.

**Attribution of the difficulty.** Beyond pass/fail, this experiment must decide which of these
the Experiment 001 ceiling was:

| Cause | What it would look like |
|---|---|
| **Configuration artefact** | `Maximum` combine makes chaining straightforwardly possible; `Average` does not. H0 holds. |
| **Insufficient control** | Chaining is possible in principle but the player cannot aim it, because air steering is too weak or too strong, or the state switch is too coarse |
| **Poor tuning** | Chaining works but only in a narrow band of speeds or angles that is hard to hit |
| **Unpredictable physics** | The same approach produces materially different outcomes run to run. Fatal: nothing can be learned against it |

The logged per-impact table is what distinguishes these. Identical approaches producing scattered
`spd_out` and `ang_out` values means unpredictable physics; consistent values that the player
still cannot exploit means a control problem.

## 6. Measurements

Per impact, from `TraversalLogger`: time, state at contact, surface hit, incoming and outgoing
speed, incoming and outgoing angle relative to the contact normal, the horizontal/vertical split
of each, and the apex reached after the impact.

Per attempt: number of impacts, peak height, which targets were reached and after how many
impacts, the active variant, and the physics configuration in force.

The instrumentation is deliberately narrower than a full trace. The horizontal/vertical split and
the two angles are the columns that answer H1: a conversion is working when a large `h_in`
becomes a large `v_out` and the following apex exceeds the height the ball came from.

## 7. Implementation

| File | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| `Scripts/BounceTraversalController.cs` | Movement and state input. Separate ground/air forces; speed clamp applies only when grounded; rubber is hold-to-activate |
| `Scripts/BounceTarget.cs` | One trigger volume, binary reached/not-reached per attempt |
| `Scripts/TraversalRunner.cs` | Attempt lifecycle, reset, and the live combine-mode variant swap |
| `Scripts/Instrumentation/TraversalLogger.cs` | Per-impact records and the per-attempt summary. Observation only |
| `Editor/Experiment002ChamberBuilder.cs` | Generates the chamber, materials and wiring |

`PhysicsStateSwitcher` is **reused unchanged from Experiment 001**, because it is the same idea
here and duplicating it would create two drifting definitions of what "rubber" means. One method,
`OverrideMaterial`, was added to it for the variant swap; Experiment 001's behaviour is unaffected.
This is a real dependency from `Experiment002` onto `Experiment001` and is taken knowingly.

`TraversalLogger` is a separate class rather than an extension of `BounceLogger`, because that one
is organised around a vertical drop where apex height is the measurement and every impact is
head-on. Here the angles and the horizontal/vertical split are the point.

No custom bounce impulses. Restitution, friction and contact resolution remain entirely PhysX.

## 8. Assumptions and unverified claims

Unity is not available on the machine where this was implemented, so **none of it has been
compiled or run**.

1. **The combine-mode priority rule.** Everything in H0 rests on PhysX resolving a mismatched
   pair by taking the higher-priority mode (`Average` < `Minimum` < `Multiply` < `Maximum`) rather
   than averaging the modes. The variant swap tests this directly: if `Maximum` changes nothing,
   this assumption is wrong and H0 collapses.
2. **That the effective restitution in Experiment 001 was 0.425.** Inferred from the configuration
   and from the two-bounce observation matching the prediction. Not measured. The
   `e = spd_out / spd_in` column on a head-on impact will confirm or refute it directly.
3. **Platform positions.** Computed from a frictionless reflection model at 12 m/s into a 30°
   ramp. Real contacts lose tangential speed to friction and convert some into spin, so real
   landings will fall short. Rubber's dynamic friction of 0.9 makes this a large effect on an
   angled impact, and it may prove to be the dominant limitation.
4. **That the ball can reach 12 m/s on the shorter platforms.** At 25 N on 1 kg it needs about
   2.9 m; `Platform_A` gives 8. Should be sufficient; unverified.
5. **Whether spin materially changes the launch.** A high-friction sphere hitting a ramp will
   pick up angular velocity, and the angular/linear exchange is not modelled in any prediction
   here. This is the most likely source of a surprise.
6. **The Unity 6 API names** and the Physics Material asset extension remain unconfirmed, as in
   Experiment 001.
7. **`OnTriggerEnter` firing reliably at launch speeds.** Continuous collision detection is
   enabled for solid contacts, but trigger volumes are 1.6 units tall against ~24 cm of travel
   per step, so this should be safe. Unverified.
